Oil, inflation and the Fed: why gold is struggling despite geopolitical risk

When conflict pushes oil higher, many investors expect gold to rise automatically. Late October 2026 shows why the real chain of cause and effect is more complicated.

The simple story broke down

The familiar story says uncertainty creates fear, fear creates safe-haven demand, and safe-haven demand lifts gold. Yet gold fell sharply in late October while Middle East tensions and oil prices rose. Reuters reported that bullion reached a three-month low as the oil rally revived inflation fears and expectations of tighter US monetary policy.

That does not mean gold has stopped being a haven. It means several forces were pulling at the same time. Some investors wanted protection from geopolitical risk. Others saw higher energy costs, more persistent inflation and a Federal Reserve with less room to cut rates. Still others were selling liquid holdings to cover losses elsewhere. The market price reflected the balance, not a single headline.

Follow the chain from oil to real yields

Oil touches nearly every part of an economy. Higher fuel and transport costs can raise the price of goods, air travel, food production and industrial inputs. If businesses pass those costs to customers, inflation can remain above the central bank's target. If households expect inflation to stay high, wage and price decisions may reinforce the problem.

The Federal Reserve responds to sustained inflation with policy, not with sympathy for any asset class. Markets may anticipate higher rates or delay expected rate cuts. Nominal Treasury yields can rise. What matters most for gold is often the real yield: the return on a bond after expected inflation. Gold produces no cash flow, so a higher real return on safe bonds raises the opportunity cost of holding it.

This relationship is powerful but not perfect. Oil can also damage growth, lower consumer spending and eventually produce recession fears. In that later phase, bond yields may fall and gold may regain support. The same oil shock can therefore pressure gold first and support it later, depending on which effect dominates.

The dollar adds another layer

Gold is usually quoted in US dollars. If markets expect the Fed to be more hawkish than other central banks, the dollar can strengthen. A stronger dollar makes each ounce more expensive for buyers using other currencies, which can reduce demand at the margin. For an investor outside the United States, however, currency moves can soften or magnify the local-currency result.

This is why “gold fell” is incomplete. Gold can fall in dollars while holding up better in rupiah, euros or yen. GoldAlert separates market prices and ratios, but users should still confirm the unit, currency, source and timestamp before comparing numbers.

Why liquidity can beat the haven trade

During a broad market selloff, investors sometimes need cash immediately. Funds facing redemptions or margin calls may sell gold because it is liquid and has willing buyers. The same pattern appeared during parts of the 2008 financial crisis and the first phase of the 2020 pandemic: gold weakened during the initial cash scramble, then recovered as policy and risk conditions evolved.

The lesson is not that gold always rebounds. It is that one down day during a crisis does not prove that the safe-haven role is gone. Equally, one geopolitical shock does not guarantee a rally. A useful framework must allow gold to behave differently across the liquidity, policy and recovery phases.

A four-screen dashboard for this environment

  • Oil: is the move temporary, or are supply disruptions persisting?
  • Inflation expectations: are market-based expectations rising across several maturities?
  • Real yields and the dollar: are they moving together against gold, or sending mixed signals?
  • Positioning and flows: are ETF outflows and futures liquidation amplifying the macro story?

None of these screens predicts tomorrow. Together they make the present easier to understand and reduce the temptation to force every headline into a bullish or bearish slogan.
